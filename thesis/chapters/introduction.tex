\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

\emph{Mechanistic interpretability} seeks to reverse-engineer the internal computations of transformer language models into human-understandable terms~\citep{olah2020zoom, elhage2021mathematical}. As these models are deployed in high-stakes settings, developers are increasingly expected to demonstrate that a model is safe, to explain its failures, and to justify relying on it. A central abstraction in this effort is the \emph{circuit}: a sparse subgraph of the model's computational graph, formed by a subset of its attention heads and multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) and the connections between them, that is responsible for a specific, controlled behavior~\citep{wang2023interpretability}. Identifying the circuit that implements a given behavior is the task of \emph{circuit discovery}. What exactly counts as a circuit, and how its boundaries are drawn, remains contested, and Chapter~\ref{ch:background} treats this question in detail.

Early work identified circuits manually, through painstaking activation patching, until \citet{acdc} introduced an algorithm that automated the search. The field now offers a fast-growing set of such algorithms, each resting on its own assumptions, ablations, and evaluation protocol. This thesis concerns these automated algorithms, and specifically the question of how their outputs can be compared on equal terms.

The state of the art comprises three families, distinguished by how they estimate the importance of each edge: greedy pruning, gradient-based attribution, and gradient-based mask optimization. Automatic Circuit DisCovery (ACDC)~\citep{acdc} prunes greedily, removing edges one at a time and retaining those whose ablation shifts the model's output distribution on the task beyond a chosen threshold. Edge Attribution Patching (EAP)~\citep{syed-etal-2024-attribution} uses gradients to approximate that ablation effect, scoring every edge in a single backward pass at the cost of a first-order approximation error. Edge Pruning~\citep{edgePruning} and DiscoGP~\citep{yu-etal-2025-sheaf} also rely on gradients, but frame discovery as an optimization problem, learning a differentiable mask over the model's edges. These optimization-based methods are faster than greedy pruning and recover substantially sparser circuits that remain faithful to the full model~\citep{edgePruning, yu-etal-2025-sheaf}, which makes them the current frontier of the field.

The output of any single algorithm, however, cannot be treated as ground truth. Two findings explain why. First, there is usually no single ground-truth circuit to recover: the same behavior can be reproduced by many distinct circuits that are all equally faithful to the model, and this non-identifiability is the norm rather than the exception~\citep{meloux2025everything, chen2026rome}. Second, the faithfulness scores used to judge a circuit depend on methodological choices, in particular the ablation used to test it, so a score reflects the experimenter's setup as much as the model itself~\citep{miller2024transformer}. These findings compound. Each algorithm is published with its own ablation, metric, and evaluation code, so a reported difference between two algorithms cannot be attributed to the algorithms themselves rather than to the protocols under which they were measured. Evaluating them under one fixed protocol, with identical ablations and identical metrics on identical tasks, is a precondition for any claim about their relative performance.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}

The motivation above gives rise to two coupled gaps. The first is a tooling gap. The optimization-based methods, Edge Pruning and DiscoGP, are distributed as standalone implementations, each carrying its own evaluation procedure. AutoCircuit~\citep{miller2024transformer}, a standard framework for edge-level circuit evaluation, does not support them. Without a shared pipeline, there is no way to subject all four algorithms (Edge Pruning, DiscoGP, ACDC, and EAP) to the same ablations and metrics, and therefore no controlled comparison is possible.

The second is a knowledge gap about the reliability of the optimization-based methods. Because a behavior admits many equally faithful circuits, and because the discovery procedure is itself a high-variance statistical estimator, a strong faithfulness score on a single run says little about whether a method's output is reliable. One open question is stability: repeated runs of the same method can produce substantially different circuits, so it is unclear how much of a discovered circuit is a stable property of the model rather than an artifact of one particular run. This instability has so far been quantified mainly for attribution-based methods such as EAP, and characterizing it for other discovery methods, including the mask-optimization methods studied here, is explicitly named as an open direction~\citep{meloux2025variance}. A second open question is whether a circuit reflects genuine learned structure rather than a spurious artifact. Interpretability methods are known to return plausible-looking explanations even for randomly initialized networks~\citep{meloux2025deadsalmons}, so a method must be shown to recover structure that the trained model actually contains, and not the kind of pattern it would have produced regardless. Comparing each method against a random-weight null model makes this concrete. Neither question has been examined for Edge Pruning or DiscoGP under controlled conditions, and closing the tooling gap is the precondition for answering both, since each requires evaluating every method under identical conditions.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:rqs}

This thesis integrates Edge Pruning and DiscoGP, in its edge-only configuration, into the AutoCircuit library, enabling a rigorous and controlled comparison against the algorithms the library already provides. The comparison is carried out on small, well-studied transformer models, GPT-2~\citep{gpt2} and a four-layer attention-only model, across three established benchmark tasks: Indirect Object Identification (IOI)~\citep{wang2023interpretability}, Greater-Than~\citep{hanna2023doesgpt2computegreaterthan}, and Docstring completion~\citep{stefanhex_janiak_2023}. The central research question is:

\begin{quote}
  \textbf{How do state-of-the-art optimization-based circuit discovery methods compare against established algorithms when integrated into a common evaluation framework and assessed under identical ablations and metrics?}
\end{quote}

\noindent To address this question systematically, the investigation is structured around three sub-questions:

\begin{description}
  \item[Sub-RQ 1 (Integration).] How can Edge Pruning and DiscoGP be integrated into the edge-centric architecture of the AutoCircuit library, so that they are evaluated under the same ablations and metrics as the algorithms it already provides (ACDC and Edge Attribution Patching)?
  \item[Sub-RQ 2 (Faithfulness).] Under identical ablations and metrics, how do the integrated methods compare against the established algorithms (ACDC and EAP) in faithfulness across the benchmark tasks (IOI, Greater-Than, and Docstring)?
  \item[Sub-RQ 3 (Reliability).] How reliable are the resulting circuits, in terms of their stability across repeated runs and whether they reflect genuine learned structure rather than spurious artifacts, tested against a random-weight null model?
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A unified, open-source integration of Edge Pruning and DiscoGP into the AutoCircuit framework, the first setting in which these optimization-based methods are evaluated under the same ablations and metrics as the established ACDC and EAP algorithms.
  \item A controlled, methodology-aware comparison of all four algorithms across three benchmark tasks, holding ablation and metric choices fixed so that the observed differences reflect the algorithms themselves rather than their evaluation setups.
  \item An empirical characterization of the methods along three axes (faithfulness, run-to-run stability, and behavior against a random-weight null model), addressing not only which method is most faithful, but also how stable each method's circuits are and whether they reflect genuine learned structure.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Outline}\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:background} introduces the necessary background on transformers, the circuit abstraction, and the AutoCircuit framework. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets these findings and discusses their limitations, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes and outlines directions for future work.
