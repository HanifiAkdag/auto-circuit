\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

Transformer language models have become remarkably capable, yet how they arrive at their outputs remains largely opaque. \emph{Mechanistic interpretability} addresses this opacity by attempting to reverse-engineer the internal algorithms a model has learned, expressing them in terms a human can inspect~\citep{elhage2021mathematical}. A central abstraction in this effort is the \emph{circuit}: the sparse subgraph of model components (attention heads and multi-layer perceptrons, or MLPs) and the connections between them that are jointly responsible for a specific, well-defined behavior~\citep{wang2023interpretability}. \emph{Circuit discovery} is the task of identifying that subgraph automatically, replacing the painstaking manual analysis of early work with reproducible algorithms. This thesis studies circuit discovery on GPT-2 Small~\citep{gpt2}, using three established benchmark tasks: Indirect Object Identification (IOI)~\citep{wang2023interpretability}, Greater-Than~\citep{hanna2023doesgpt2computegreaterthan}, and Docstring completion~\citep{stefanhex_janiak_2023}.

The state of the art in circuit discovery spans three families of methods. \emph{Search-based} methods, exemplified by Automatic Circuit Discovery (ACDC)~\citep{acdc}, iteratively test and prune connections to isolate those that matter for the task. \emph{Attribution-based} methods, such as Edge Attribution Patching (EAP)~\citep{syed-etal-2024-attribution}, estimate the importance of every connection in a single pass, trading some accuracy for a large gain in speed. Most recently, \emph{gradient-based} methods, such as Edge Pruning~\citep{edgePruning} and DiscoGP~\citep{yu-etal-2025-sheaf}, frame discovery as an optimization problem, learning a differentiable mask over the model's connections. These optimization methods are faster than exhaustive search and recover sparser, more faithful circuits, which makes them the current frontier of the field.

Circuit discovery is increasingly relied upon to support claims about model safety, debugging, and trustworthiness, so the \emph{reliability} of the circuits it produces matters a great deal. Two findings complicate this picture. First, a discovered circuit is not a unique ground truth: the result depends heavily on methodological choices such as the ablation used to test it, so faithfulness scores reflect the experimenter's setup as much as the model~\citep{miller2024transformer}. Second, a single task generally admits \emph{many} distinct circuits that are each equally faithful, a non-identifiability that is intrinsic to the problem rather than a defect of any one algorithm~\citep{meloux2025deadsalmons}. Together these mean that we cannot uncritically trust any single method's output and, more pressingly, that we cannot even \emph{compare} methods fairly today. Each algorithm is typically published with its own evaluation framework, its own ablation strategy, and its own codebase, so apparent differences between methods are confounded by differences in how they were measured. Establishing a fair basis for comparison is the gap this thesis sets out to close.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}

The motivation above gives rise to two coupled gaps. The first is a \emph{tooling} gap. The gradient-based methods, Edge Pruning and DiscoGP, are distributed as standalone implementations, each carrying its own evaluation procedure. AutoCircuit~\citep{miller2024transformer}, a standard framework for edge-level circuit evaluation, does not support them. Without a shared pipeline, there is no way to subject all four algorithms (Edge Pruning, DiscoGP, ACDC, and EAP) to the same ablations and metrics, and therefore no controlled comparison is possible.

The second is a \emph{knowledge} gap. Existing comparisons of circuit-discovery algorithms tend to conflate the algorithm with the methodology used to evaluate it, and they are usually based on a single run. This leaves two questions unanswered for the gradient-based methods in particular: how \emph{stable} are their circuits across repeated runs, and do they recover genuine task structure at all, as opposed to plausible-looking artifacts, a question made concrete by comparing against a \emph{random-weight null model}. Closing the tooling gap is the precondition for answering these questions, since both require evaluating every method under identical conditions.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:rqs}

This thesis integrates Edge Pruning and DiscoGP, in its edge-only configuration, into the AutoCircuit library, enabling a rigorous and controlled comparison against the algorithms the library already provides. The central research question is:

\begin{quote}
  \textbf{How do state-of-the-art gradient-based circuit discovery methods compare against established algorithms when integrated into a common evaluation framework and assessed under identical ablations and metrics?}
\end{quote}

\noindent To address this question systematically, the investigation is structured around three sub-questions:

\begin{description}
  \item[Sub-RQ 1 (Integration).] How can Edge Pruning and DiscoGP be integrated into the edge-centric architecture of the AutoCircuit library, so that they are evaluated under the same ablations and metrics as the algorithms it already provides (ACDC, Edge Attribution Patching, and subnetwork probing)?
  \item[Sub-RQ 2 (Faithfulness).] Under identical ablations and metrics, how do the integrated methods compare against the established algorithms (ACDC and EAP) in faithfulness across the benchmark tasks (IOI, Greater-Than, and Docstring)?
  \item[Sub-RQ 3 (Reliability).] How reliable are the resulting circuits: how stable are they across repeated runs, and do they recover meaningful structure relative to a random-weight null model?
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A unified, open-source integration of Edge Pruning and DiscoGP into the AutoCircuit framework, the first setting in which these gradient-based methods are evaluated under the same ablations and metrics as the established ACDC and EAP algorithms.
  \item A controlled, methodology-aware comparison of all four algorithms across three benchmark tasks, holding ablation and metric choices fixed so that the observed differences reflect the algorithms themselves rather than their evaluation setups.
  \item An empirical characterization of the methods along three axes (faithfulness, run-to-run stability, and behavior against a random-weight null model), addressing not only which method is most faithful, but how reliable and non-spurious each method's circuits are.
\end{itemize}
