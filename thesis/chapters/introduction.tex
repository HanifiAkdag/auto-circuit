\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

\emph{Mechanistic interpretability} aims to open the black box of transformer language models by reverse-engineering the circuits behind their behavior; as these models are deployed in high-stakes settings, it is increasingly asked to show that a model is safe, to explain its failures, and to justify trusting it~\citep{elhage2021mathematical}. Yet the field now offers a fast-growing set of circuit-discovery algorithms, each with its own assumptions, ablations, and evaluation, so it is remarkably hard to say which one to trust, or even whether they would agree on the same task. A central abstraction in this effort is the \emph{circuit}, understood broadly as a small subset of model components (such as attention heads and MLPs) together with the connections between them that account for a specific, well-defined behavior~\citep{wang2023interpretability}. Exactly what should count as a circuit, and how its boundaries are drawn, is itself an open question that we return to in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}. \emph{Circuit discovery} is then the task of finding such a subset for a given behavior. Early work did this by hand, through painstaking activation patching, until \citet{acdc} introduced an algorithm to automate the search; this thesis concerns the automated, algorithmic approaches that followed. We work with small, well-studied transformer models, namely GPT-2 Small~\citep{gpt2} and a four-layer attention-only model, on three established benchmark tasks: Indirect Object Identification (IOI)~\citep{wang2023interpretability}, Greater-Than~\citep{hanna2023doesgpt2computegreaterthan}, and Docstring completion~\citep{stefanhex_janiak_2023}.

The state of the art in circuit discovery spans three families of methods. Search-based methods, exemplified by Automatic Circuit Discovery (ACDC)~\citep{acdc}, iteratively test and prune connections to isolate those that matter for the task. Attribution-based methods, such as Edge Attribution Patching (EAP)~\citep{syed-etal-2024-attribution}, estimate the importance of every connection in a single pass, trading some accuracy for a large gain in speed. Most recently, gradient-based methods, such as Edge Pruning~\citep{edgePruning} and DiscoGP~\citep{yu-etal-2025-sheaf}, frame discovery as an optimization problem, learning a differentiable mask over the model's connections. These optimization methods are faster than exhaustive search and recover sparser, more faithful circuits, which makes them the current frontier of the field~\citep{yu-etal-2025-sheaf}.

Yet none of these methods can be taken at face value. Two findings show why the reliability of any single one's output is not guaranteed. First, a discovered circuit is not a unique ground truth: a single behavior generally admits many distinct circuits that are each equally faithful, a non-identifiability that has been shown to be the norm rather than the exception~\citep{meloux2025everything}. Second, even the faithfulness scores used to judge a circuit depend heavily on methodological choices such as the ablation used to test it, so they reflect the experimenter's setup as much as the model itself~\citep{miller2024transformer}. Together these mean that we cannot uncritically trust any single method's output and, more pressingly, that we cannot even compare methods fairly today. Because each is evaluated on its own terms, apparent differences between methods are confounded by differences in how they were measured. Establishing a fair basis for comparison is the gap this thesis sets out to close.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}

The motivation above gives rise to two coupled gaps. The first is a tooling gap. The gradient-based methods, Edge Pruning and DiscoGP, are distributed as standalone implementations, each carrying its own evaluation procedure. AutoCircuit~\citep{miller2024transformer}, a standard framework for edge-level circuit evaluation, does not support them. Without a shared pipeline, there is no way to subject all four algorithms (Edge Pruning, DiscoGP, ACDC, and EAP) to the same ablations and metrics, and therefore no controlled comparison is possible.

The second is a knowledge gap about the reliability of the gradient-based methods. Because a behavior admits many equally faithful circuits, and because the discovery procedure is itself a high-variance statistical estimator, a strong faithfulness score on a single run says little about whether a method's output can be trusted. Two reliability questions therefore remain open for Edge Pruning and DiscoGP, neither of which has been examined for them under controlled conditions. The first is stability: repeated runs of the same method can produce substantially different circuits, so it is unclear how much of a discovered circuit is a stable property of the model rather than an artifact of one particular run. This instability has so far been quantified mainly for attribution-based methods such as EAP, and characterizing it for other discovery methods, including the gradient-based optimizers studied here, is explicitly named as an open direction~\citep{meloux2025variance}. The second is whether a circuit reflects genuine learned structure rather than a spurious artifact: interpretability methods are known to return plausible-looking explanations even for randomly initialized networks~\citep{meloux2025deadsalmons}, so a method ought to be shown to recover real structure and not the kind of pattern it would have produced regardless. Comparing each method against a random-weight null model makes this concrete. Closing the tooling gap is the precondition for answering both questions, since each requires evaluating every method under identical conditions.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:rqs}

This thesis integrates Edge Pruning and DiscoGP, in its edge-only configuration, into the AutoCircuit library, enabling a rigorous and controlled comparison against the algorithms the library already provides. The central research question is:

\begin{quote}
  \textbf{How do state-of-the-art gradient-based circuit discovery methods compare against established algorithms when integrated into a common evaluation framework and assessed under identical ablations and metrics?}
\end{quote}

\noindent To address this question systematically, the investigation is structured around three sub-questions:

\begin{description}
  \item[Sub-RQ 1 (Integration).] How can Edge Pruning and DiscoGP be integrated into the edge-centric architecture of the AutoCircuit library, so that they are evaluated under the same ablations and metrics as the algorithms it already provides (ACDC, Edge Attribution Patching, and subnetwork probing)?
  \item[Sub-RQ 2 (Faithfulness).] Under identical ablations and metrics, how do the integrated methods compare against the established algorithms (ACDC and EAP) in faithfulness across the benchmark tasks (IOI, Greater-Than, and Docstring)?
  \item[Sub-RQ 3 (Reliability).] How reliable are the resulting circuits, in terms of their stability across repeated runs and whether they reflect genuine learned structure rather than spurious artifacts, tested against a random-weight null model?
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A unified, open-source integration of Edge Pruning and DiscoGP into the AutoCircuit framework, the first setting in which these gradient-based methods are evaluated under the same ablations and metrics as the established ACDC and EAP algorithms.
  \item A controlled, methodology-aware comparison of all four algorithms across three benchmark tasks, holding ablation and metric choices fixed so that the observed differences reflect the algorithms themselves rather than their evaluation setups.
  \item An empirical characterization of the methods along three axes (faithfulness, run-to-run stability, and behavior against a random-weight null model), addressing not only which method is most faithful, but how reliable and non-spurious each method's circuits are.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Outline}\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:background} introduces the necessary background on transformers, the circuit abstraction, and the AutoCircuit framework. Chapter~\ref{ch:related-work} surveys related work on circuit-discovery algorithms and on the reliability of circuit faithfulness. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets these findings and discusses their limitations, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes and outlines directions for future work.
